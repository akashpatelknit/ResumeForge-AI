An "ATS score" is a number — usually a percentage or a match rating — that an Applicant Tracking System assigns to your resume when it's compared against a specific job posting. It's not one universal standard; different platforms (Workday, Greenhouse, Lever, Taleo, and dozens of others) calculate it differently. But the underlying idea is consistent enough that understanding it is worth your time.

## What the score is actually measuring

At its core, an ATS score is a similarity measure between your resume and the job description. Most systems weigh some combination of:

- **Keyword overlap** — how many of the skills, tools, and qualifications named in the posting also appear in your resume
- **Title and seniority match** — whether your listed job titles and years of experience line up with what the role expects
- **Section completeness** — whether the system could reliably find and parse standard fields (contact info, work history, education, skills)
- **Recency and relevance** — some systems weight your most recent roles more heavily than older ones

A resume can be genuinely well-written and still score low if it uses different vocabulary than the posting, or if formatting issues prevented the system from parsing it correctly in the first place. This is the most important thing to internalize: **ATS score is not a proxy for how good a candidate you are.** It's a proxy for how well your resume's text matches a specific posting's text. Those are related but different things.

## Why it matters anyway

Recruiters at companies using an ATS frequently sort or filter applicants by score rather than reading every resume that comes in — especially for high-volume postings that get hundreds of applicants in the first day. If your resume scores low, the practical effect is the same as if a human rejected it: nobody reads it. It doesn't matter how strong your actual experience is if the resume never surfaces.

This is also why the same resume can score very differently across two postings for what looks like the same job. A "Product Manager" posting at one company might emphasize "roadmap" and "stakeholder alignment," while another emphasizes "A/B testing" and "growth metrics." A resume tuned for the first will score lower against the second, even though both are legitimately "product manager" experience.

## How to actually raise it

1. **Tailor per application.** A generic resume sent to fifty postings will score inconsistently across all of them. Adjust the terminology in your skills and experience bullets to reflect each specific posting's language, without fabricating experience you don't have.
2. **Match both the concept and the exact phrase.** If a posting says "cross-functional collaboration," don't assume "worked with other teams" will register as equivalent in every system — where it's true, use language close to the posting's own.
3. **Keep formatting simple.** A perfectly worded resume that a parser can't read correctly will score as if those sections are missing. See our companion post on [ATS-safe formatting](/blog/how-to-write-a-resume-that-passes-ats-screening) for the specifics.
4. **Don't over-optimize into dishonesty.** Listing skills you don't have to chase a higher score gets caught in the interview, and some newer ATS platforms now flag likely keyword stuffing as a negative signal rather than a positive one.

## What a "good" score looks like

There's no universal passing threshold — it depends on the platform, the role, and how competitive the applicant pool is. As a general rule of thumb, treat a low match score as a signal to investigate, not a verdict. If a posting closely matches roles you've held and your score still comes back low, that's usually a formatting or vocabulary mismatch worth fixing, not a sign you're unqualified.
