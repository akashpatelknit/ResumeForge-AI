Referred candidates get interviewed at a meaningfully higher rate than cold applicants at most companies — some internal studies from large employers put it several times higher. Recruiters trust their own employees' judgment more than a resume alone, and a referral usually means someone internal is willing to vouch, at least a little, for your competence. Despite this, most job seekers under-ask for referrals, because it feels like imposing on a relationship for personal gain.

The discomfort is real, but it's solvable with the right framing and the right ask. Here's how to do it well.

## Reframe what you're actually asking for

The instinct is to think "I'm asking them to get me a job." That's not accurate, and it's the wrong frame — it puts unnecessary pressure on both of you. What you're actually asking for is much smaller: *"Will you submit my resume through the internal referral system?"* That's usually a two-minute action on their end, often with an upside for them too (many companies pay referral bonuses). You are not asking them to vouch for your work with their reputation, guarantee you an interview, or advocate for you in a hiring meeting — unless they offer to do more, which some will.

Naming this distinction, even just to yourself, makes the ask feel proportionate instead of presumptuous.

## Only ask people who can say yes easily

Don't lead with your closest mentor or your former manager for a first-degree ask — lead with whoever has the lowest-friction "yes." A former coworker, a college classmate, someone you've had a few good conversations with on LinkedIn in a relevant field — these are lower-stakes asks than someone who'd feel obligated to have a longer conversation about your candidacy first.

If you don't have any existing connection at a company, a cold outreach *can* work, but treat it differently — see the note at the end.

## Use a message that does the work for them

The biggest reason people don't refer someone isn't reluctance — it's friction. If your ask requires them to read your whole resume, figure out what role fits, and dig up the internal portal link themselves, it often just doesn't happen, not out of unwillingness but out of a busy week. Remove every piece of friction you can:

> Hey [Name] — hope you're doing well! I saw [Company] has an opening for [exact role title, with a link] and I think it's a strong fit for my background in [one line: relevant experience]. Would you be open to referring me if you think it makes sense? Happy to send my resume over, and totally no worries if the timing or fit isn't right.

Notice what this does:
- Names the exact role, so they don't have to search for it
- Gives one sentence of "why me," so they don't have to guess whether it's a fit
- Offers an easy out ("totally no worries") so saying no — or not responding — doesn't feel like it damages the relationship
- Doesn't ask them to write a paragraph of endorsement, just to submit

Attach your resume (or a link to it) in the same message rather than making them ask for it.

## Follow up once, then let it go

If you don't hear back in about a week, one light follow-up is reasonable — people are busy, and messages get buried. After that, drop it. Repeated follow-ups turn a low-friction favor into a source of guilt, which is exactly the dynamic that makes people avoid referring others in the future.

## When you don't have a connection yet

If there's no existing relationship at the company, you can still reach out — but the ask should change. Don't open with a referral request to a stranger; open with genuine interest in their work or a specific, well-researched question. A referral ask to someone who doesn't know you at all, as a first message, has a low response rate and can read as transactional. If a real conversation develops, a referral ask later on lands far better than leading with it.

## Why this is worth the discomfort

A referral doesn't guarantee an offer, and it doesn't replace a resume and interview performance that hold up on their own. What it does is get you past the part of the process where most applications quietly disappear. Combined with a resume that's actually tailored to the role, a referral is one of the few levers in a job search that meaningfully shifts your odds — and asking for one, done well, costs the other person less than most people assume.
